Aiden Iyer reporting in for Thursday. Today's channel brief, kept tight — five things worth raising on calls today.

First up, Oracle. CISA has issued an emergency directive ordering federal agencies to lock down Oracle E-Business Suite by this Saturday, because a critical flaw in that financial application is already being exploited in the wild, not just theorized. Federal customers are on a hard, dated clock, and that urgency doesn't stop at the .gov boundary — plenty of commercial E-Business Suite shops are exposed too. If you carry Oracle accounts, this is your excuse to call today, not next week. Ask whether they've hit that deadline, and if not, that's a clean advisory opener: an emergency patch and configuration review, or a pentest engagement to confirm they're not already exposed.

Following that, the Scattered Spider story that's been all over mainstream news, not just the trade press. Two members of that hacking group were sentenced to five and a half years for the twenty twenty four ransomware attack on Transport for London. It hit BBC, TechCrunch, and pretty much every security outlet at once, so expect customers to bring this one up unprompted, the way they did with prior Scattered Spider hits on casinos and retailers. Their playbook is help desk social engineering and multifactor bypass, not some exotic zero-day, which makes it a natural lead into an identity and help desk hardening conversation, or a social engineering assessment if you sell pentest services. Worth noting too: industry data out this week shows identity attacks have now overtaken exploit-based intrusions as the top root cause of ransomware, so this isn't just one headline, it's the trend.

Next, a double patch day worth flagging. Splunk and Zoom both shipped critical security updates this week. The Zoom flaw, tracked as CVE-2026-53412, is a nine point eight out of ten severity bug in the Windows desktop client, the VDI client, and the Meeting SDK, and an unauthenticated attacker could use it to hijack accounts outright. That's a same day patch conversation for nearly every customer, since everyone runs Zoom somewhere in their stack. Separately, Splunk closed its own set of credential access and privilege escalation bugs. If you carry Splunk, this is a good moment to check in with your top renewals and make sure patching is actually landing, not just scheduled.

Fourth, F five. They patched multiple critical vulnerabilities across NGINX and BIG IP that could let an attacker modify configurations, terminate or restart processes, cross security boundaries, and leak memory. F five has a track record of high profile incidents, so any BIG IP account is worth a proactive call before they see the headline elsewhere. A configuration and hardening review is an easy advisory pitch here, and if a customer sounds fatigued with F five's patch cadence, it's also a fair opening for a competitive, displacement conversation.

Last one today is out of Japan. A cyberattack on Nichirei Logistics, the country's largest cold chain logistics operator, has left KFC restaurants short on ingredients and other major chains struggling to keep supermarket shelves stocked. It's a clean, named example of how one logistics vendor getting hit cascades straight into retail and food service outages within days. Use it as the operational technology and supply chain risk opener: if a single cold chain operator going down can dent a fast food giant's supply, what does a comparable hit do to your customer's operations? That's an easy bridge into an OT security or business continuity advisory conversation, especially with accounts who've never had that assessment done.

That's your brief. Aiden out — go close something.
