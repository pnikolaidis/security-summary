Good morning. It's Friday. Avery Ito with the week's biggest moves in the channel — five things worth raising on calls today, and a couple of them come with hard deadlines, so don't let this one sit in your inbox over the weekend.

First up, Fortinet. CISA ordered federal agencies to patch two actively exploited flaws in Fortinet's FortiSandbox threat detection platform, and the deadline is this Sunday. These aren't theoretical bugs sitting in a disclosure report — they're already being used in attacks right now, which is exactly why CISA moved this fast. If you're carrying Fortinet, this is your excuse to call every FortiSandbox account today, not next week. Don't wait for their normal patch cycle. It's an easy pentest opener: offer to validate exposure before that Sunday deadline hits. It's also a natural lead into a broader conversation about their patch management process, and if detection and response coverage around that sandbox platform looks thin, that's your expand angle too.

Second, Microsoft SharePoint. CISA added a critical SharePoint zero-day, rated nine-point-eight out of ten on the severity scale, to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog after confirming it's being actively exploited, in some cases within days of disclosure. Federal agencies have until July nineteenth to patch. It's a deserialization flaw that lets a remote, authenticated attacker run arbitrary code on the server — exactly the kind of foothold ransomware crews love to stage from. Any customer still running SharePoint Server on-premises needs to hear about this today, government deadline or not. It's a strong pentest angle: offer an external attack surface check to see if they're actually exposed. And if they're sitting on an aging on-prem SharePoint deployment, that's also your reposition opener for a cloud or Zero Trust alternative.

Third, Ernst and Young. The Big Four accounting and advisory firm disclosed a data breach traced back to a compromised third-party support ticket system used by its own I-T staff. Scope details are still emerging, but the pattern is a familiar one: a support tool with broad internal access became the way in. This is a great low-pressure opener for vendor risk conversations — even customers who've never touched E-Y will recognize themselves in this story, because almost everyone runs some outsourced support or helpdesk tool with more access than anyone's audited lately. Good advisory angle here is a third-party risk assessment, and there's an upsell sitting right behind it: privileged access controls on their own support and helpdesk tooling.

Next, Coca-Cola. A ransomware attack against its Fairlife dairy subsidiary has halted U-S production, and as of this morning the company still hasn't determined the full scope of the incident. Plants in Michigan, New York, and Arizona are affected. This one's already mainstream news — it's not buried in trade press, it's the kind of headline your manufacturing and C-P-G customers will already be asking their own teams about. Lead with operational technology and I-T segmentation. Pitch an incident response retainer or an O-T security posture review, because this is exactly the kind of story that makes budget appear out of nowhere on a Friday afternoon.

Last, CMMC. The Pentagon suspended Phase 2 of its Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification program, and a lot of defense contractors are breathing a sigh of relief this week. Don't let them relax too much on your watch — the suspension only pauses third-party audits, it does not remove the underlying legal obligation to protect controlled unclassified information under existing contract clauses. That gap between "audit paused" and "requirement still active" is exactly your opening. Sell a CMMC or N-I-S-T eight hundred dash one seventy one readiness assessment now, while the pressure feels soft but the requirement hasn't actually gone anywhere — and position it as a renewal conversation before the audits come back and everyone's scrambling at once.

That's your brief. Five calls worth making today: two patch deadlines, one vendor-risk opener, one headline your customers already saw, and one compliance window that won't stay open forever. Avery out — go close something.
