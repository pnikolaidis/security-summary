Good morning. Here is your security digest for June 5th, 2026. It's Friday. Avery Ito with what mattered this week. Today's signal has three items that call for immediate action, one that signals an expanding geopolitical threat, and one piece of research that is already reshaping how the security community thinks about AI-assisted vulnerability hunting.

Let's start with the most urgent.

Story one. Cisco is dealing with its seventh SD-WAN zero-day this year, and this one — tracked as CVE-2026-20245 — has no patch and is already being exploited in the wild. An unauthenticated attacker who can reach a vulnerable device over the network can gain root access. That means full control. Cisco's SD-WAN gear sits at the edge of enterprise networks and government installations, which makes this particularly serious. Cisco has published workaround guidance, but the patch is not out yet. If you run SD-WAN infrastructure, you should treat your telemetry as potentially tainted, apply any available mitigations today, and watch for lateral movement. Seven zero-days in one product line in a single year is not a coincidence. Something is structurally wrong there, and attackers clearly know it.

Story two. Google pushed a major Chrome security update this week, version 149.0.7827.53, patching thirty-seven vulnerabilities. Most of the security story is about two of them: CVE-2026-10881 and CVE-2026-10886, both rated critical at a CVSS score of 9.6. These are what researchers call sandbox-escape primitives. A regular Chrome flaw lets an attacker run code inside the browser. A sandbox-escape flaw means an attacker who controls a malicious web page could potentially break out of Chrome entirely and run code on your actual operating system. That is a different order of threat. The two critical bugs are in the ANGLE graphics layer and the FileSystem API. Beyond those two, there are dozens of use-after-free and type-confusion bugs in V8, WebRTC, and other subsystems. Chrome's auto-update handles this for most personal devices, but enterprise fleet managers should verify that deployed versions have been updated and that any browser-pinning policies are not blocking the patch.

Story three. A new supply-chain attack called IronWorm is targeting developers through the npm registry. Researchers found thirty-six malicious packages designed to look like legitimate utilities. When installed, IronWorm — written in Rust, which makes it harder for conventional detection tools to flag — quietly harvests API keys, cloud credentials, SSH keys, and environment variables, and sends them to attacker-controlled servers. If you or your CI/CD pipeline ran npm install recently, you should cross-reference your package lock files against the list of compromised packages and rotate any secrets that may have been exposed. The packages are being removed from npm, but removal does not undo the theft if the code already ran on your system.

Story four. The Chinese threat group known as TA4922 is expanding. Three separate outlets reported this week that the group, which has been running record-volume phishing campaigns throughout 2026, has now extended its targeting to the United Kingdom, Germany, Italy, and South Africa, having previously focused on North American and Asia-Pacific victims. The campaigns use fake job offers and convincing professional lures, typically delivered through LinkedIn-style documents. The primary targets are government personnel, military staff, and defense-adjacent organizations. A joint advisory from Five Eyes intelligence agencies is expected. If your organization has facilities or staff in any of the newly targeted regions, this is a good moment to refresh phishing awareness training and verify that phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication is enabled on all externally accessible systems.

Story five. Anthropic published an open-source framework this week designed to help security researchers use AI models to automatically find and triage vulnerabilities in code. The project hit the top of Hacker News with nearly five hundred upvotes, which is a signal the security community is paying close attention. The framework combines AI-guided fuzzing hints, static analysis augmentation, and automated attempts to sketch proof-of-concept reproductions. The conversation in the comments and on social media has quickly turned to the dual-use question: the same tooling that helps defenders scan their own codebases could also be adapted by offensive researchers to find exploitable bugs in other people's software. Anthropic has framed the project as a defensive and research tool for authorized security work. Whether that framing holds depends on how the community adopts it. This one is worth watching.

That's your digest. Stay safe out there.
