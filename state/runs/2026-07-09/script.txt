Good morning. Aiden Iyer reporting in for Thursday. Today's channel brief, kept tight: five things worth raising on calls today, running the gamut from a hot AI vendor research story to a regulatory deadline getting teeth. It was a quieter overnight cycle for named breaches, but the AI-agent security story alone is worth the price of admission, so let's get into it.

First up, Wiz. Their researchers found a flaw they're calling GhostApproval that hits six popular AI coding assistants at once, including Amazon's Q Developer and Google's Antigravity, along with Claude Code, Cursor, Windsurf, and Augment. The trick is simple but nasty: the agent asks permission to edit one harmless-looking file, but a symlink swap means the write actually lands somewhere sensitive. If you've got customers rolling out AI coding assistants, this is your opener. Ask whether anyone's actually reviewed how those approval prompts work under the hood, because Wiz just showed the answer is probably not. That's a clean advisory play, a governance review of their AI-assisted development pipeline, and honestly a good pentest hook too, since you can go test their agent setup directly.

Next, Brussels is done waiting. The European Commission just referred Ireland, Spain, France, and the Netherlands to the EU Court of Justice for failing to properly implement the NIS2 directive. If you've got customers operating in or selling into the EU who've been treating NIS2 as a someday problem, this is your someday-is-now moment. That's a straightforward advisory opener, a NIS2 gap assessment, and depending on their risk profile, it can pull through pentest work too, since NIS2's risk-management rules basically require it.

Following that, there's a vishing campaign making the rounds that specifically targets Entra passkey enrollment. Attackers are calling Microsoft 365 users, walking them through registering a new passkey, and the passkey that gets registered belongs to the attacker, not the user. It's a good reminder that passkeys don't automatically stop social engineering, they just move the target to the enrollment step. Worth asking Microsoft 365 customers point blank whether their help desk verifies identity before approving new passkey or MFA registrations. That's an advisory play on help-desk hardening, and it opens the door to an upsell on phishing-resistant MFA paired with better registration controls.

Fourth, KDDI, the Japanese telecom carrier, disclosed a breach touching around 12 million customer records. Details on exactly how attackers got in haven't come out yet, but the scale alone makes it worth raising with any telecom account or anyone else sitting on a large subscriber database. It's the kind of story that tends to make a board ask "could that happen to us," which is exactly the conversation you want to be in the room for. Good pentest opener around their external attack surface, and it's also a nice excuse to pitch an incident-response tabletop if they haven't run one recently.

And last, Microsoft patched a zero-day in Windows Defender, tracked as CVE 2026 dash 50656, that let a local attacker escalate straight to SYSTEM privileges. Three different outlets picked this one up, and Microsoft's own advisory says they're aware of proof of concept exploitation already circulating. If you're carrying competing EDR product, this is a good "how fast can you actually patch" conversation to have with Defender shops, especially if their patch management process is manual or lags behind release day. And if they are on Defender, it's a natural opening for a patch-cadence review, and a chance to check whether their endpoint fleet is actually current or just assumed to be.
