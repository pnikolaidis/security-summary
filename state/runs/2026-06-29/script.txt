First up: Russia's Gamaredon group has expanded its cyberattack campaign against Ukraine, and ESET just published fresh research documenting thirty-five new spear-phishing campaigns. What makes this one worth bringing to your customers is the pivot — Gamaredon is now abusing legitimate cloud services for command and control. That means traditional firewall blocks and perimeter defenses aren't catching it. The group hides its infrastructure behind cloud platforms that organizations already trust, making detection harder without behavioral analysis at the network and endpoint layers. For your ESET, CrowdStrike, or Zscaler customers, this is a direct proof point: if the same techniques land outside Ukraine — and they have, against Western organizations with Ukrainian business ties — you want to be having that conversation before an incident, not after.

Second story: Microsoft has dismantled a five-year-old malicious extension operation on the Edge browser store. They call it StegoAd — a mashup of steganography and adware — and they've pulled one hundred nineteen extensions tied to a single threat actor. Here's the clever part: the attacker hid the malicious code inside ordinary image and font files embedded in the extension, then waited days after installation before activating. That delay-before-execution technique was specifically designed to bypass sandbox analysis, which looks at what software does immediately after install. The extensions were stealing credentials and running large-scale advertising fraud. For your Microsoft Defender and endpoint customers, this is a case study for why behavioral EDR and browser extension governance programs matter — traditional antivirus missed this for years. For Proofpoint customers, it reinforces the identity-theft angle. Ask your accounts: do they have visibility into which browser extensions are running across their endpoints?

Third: Japanese telecom giant KDDI confirmed a data breach that exposed up to fourteen point two million email login credentials. The attack targeted a shared email infrastructure platform that KDDI operates on behalf of five other internet service providers. One backend, six companies affected, fourteen million users potentially compromised. This is a classic shared-infrastructure cascading failure. For your email security and identity protection conversations — Proofpoint, Okta, CyberArk — this is the brief you use with telecom accounts, utilities, or any organization that operates shared infrastructure. The question to ask: if your shared platform fails, how fast do you detect it, and how do you contain the blast radius to your downstream customers?

Fourth: A public proof-of-concept exploit is now available for CVE-2026-55200, a critical vulnerability in libssh2. The CVSS score is nine point two. libssh2 is a client-side SSH library embedded in cURL, countless automation tools, network appliances, and server-side platforms — it's the kind of dependency that gets forgotten about in software inventories. The flaw lets any malicious or compromised SSH server trigger memory corruption on a connecting client, with potential for full code execution. No credentials required, no user interaction. And because this library is bundled inside appliances and embedded systems rather than patched through standard OS update channels, the exposure window will stretch for months. For your customers: this is a software inventory and patch verification conversation. Do they know which of their internal tools or appliances are calling SSH and what version of libssh2 is underneath?

Fifth: A new Linux kernel vulnerability called DirtyClone — a variant of the DirtyFrag technique — allows any unprivileged local user to manipulate the Linux page cache and escalate to root. No CVE number or CVSS score yet, but the impact is clear: shared Linux servers, multi-tenant environments, container platforms, and cloud virtual machines with any degree of untrusted local access are at risk. Container escape is a natural next step once an attacker has kernel-level page cache manipulation. For your cloud-native accounts and anyone running Wiz, Palo Alto Prisma Cloud, or CrowdStrike Falcon for cloud workload protection — ask whether their runtime protection covers this class of kernel manipulation and whether their Linux kernel versions are current on DirtyFrag-family patches.
