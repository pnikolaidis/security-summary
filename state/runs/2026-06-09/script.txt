Good morning. This is Adam Insight with Tuesday's security digest. A pair of actively exploited zero-days leads the news today, one of them already being used by a ransomware gang, and the legal pressure on commercial spyware operators is reaching a new high.

Story one. A critical vulnerability in Check Point VPN gateways is being actively exploited by the Qilin ransomware gang, and CISA has given federal agencies just three days to apply the fix. The flaw is tracked as CVE-2026-50751 and carries a severity score of 9.3 out of 10. It affects the Remote Access and Mobile Access VPN components when configured to use certificate-based authentication with the older IKEv1 protocol. The logic flaw allows attackers to bypass password authentication entirely and gain access to corporate networks without valid credentials. Check Point confirmed that exploitation began as early as the first week of May, and Qilin — an active ransomware-as-a-service operation — has been documented using this foothold for initial access, lateral movement, and data theft across enterprise targets. Why it matters: if your organization uses Check Point VPN for remote access, this is a drop-everything, patch-before-end-of-day situation.

Story two. Google has patched its fifth actively exploited Chrome zero-day of 2026. That means threat actors have found and used a Chrome vulnerability in real attacks roughly every five to seven weeks this calendar year — a sustained pace that underscores Chrome's status as a high-priority initial access target. The vulnerability, CVE-2026-11645, is an out-of-bounds read and write in V8, Chrome's JavaScript engine, and was already being exploited in the wild before Google issued the fix. The Chrome 149 update also includes fifteen additional fixes, three of them rated critical, covering the Gamepad, Printing, and Network subsystems. Organizations running Chrome or any Chromium-based browser — including Microsoft Edge — should deploy version 149.0.7827.103 immediately. Why it matters: browser-based exploits remain one of the most reliable initial access vectors, and five zero-days in five months is a significant signal about where attacker investment is going.

Story three. Apache HTTP Server received six new CVE publications today, headlined by a CVSS 9.8 critical severity vulnerability. The most severe of the six, CVE-2026-44631, is a buffer underwrite triggered by a specially crafted HTTP request and could potentially allow unauthenticated remote code execution. The batch also includes buffer overflows in the mod_proxy_html module, heap-based buffer overflows, a buffer over-read in outbound proxy handling, and a use-after-free in the HTTP2 module. Apache is among the most widely deployed web server platforms in the world, running behind countless public-facing websites and internal enterprise systems. Administrators running version 2.4.67 or earlier should treat this as an urgent patching event, prioritizing any internet-facing instances. Why it matters: six CVEs in a single release cycle, topped by a 9.8 severity score, is one of the more significant patching moments Apache has seen in recent months.

Story four. A supply chain campaign called Hades has been caught poisoning nineteen PyPI packages to steal credentials from developer machines. The attack is an evolution of the earlier Shai-Hulud technique and specifically targets scientific computing packages — suggesting researchers, data scientists, and academic institutions are the intended victims. The malicious code auto-executes the moment a developer runs pip install, downloads the Bun JavaScript runtime in the background, and silently harvests stored credentials and secrets. PyPI has removed the affected packages, but any developer who installed Python packages in the past two weeks should audit their environment for unexpected Bun binaries and rotate any credentials that could have been exposed. Separate reporting from SANS Internet Storm Center links this activity to a broader supply chain cluster called TeamPCP that was still active as recently as June seventh. Why it matters: supply chain attacks targeting package managers are growing in sophistication, and the scientific computing community is a high-value target with historically weaker dependency governance.

Story five. Meta caught NSO Group launching a new WhatsApp spyware campaign in direct violation of a federal court injunction, and has now filed a contempt-of-court motion. NSO Group — maker of the Pegasus spyware platform — is already subject to a court order barring it from targeting WhatsApp users, the result of Meta's 2019 lawsuit. Despite that order, WhatsApp's security team discovered and disrupted a new phishing campaign deploying infrastructure designed to install surveillance software on targets' devices. Meta's contempt filing asks the court to impose sanctions on NSO, and if the court grants the motion, the consequences could significantly constrain NSO's ability to operate. The case is being watched closely as a test of whether civil litigation can meaningfully rein in commercial spyware vendors when export controls and diplomatic pressure have fallen short. Why it matters: a contempt finding against NSO would be the sharpest legal consequence the commercial spyware industry has faced and could set a precedent that reshapes how these vendors calculate risk.

That's your digest for Tuesday, June ninth. Prioritize the Check Point VPN and Chrome patches today, schedule the Apache update, audit your PyPI dependencies if you run Python environments, and watch the NSO contempt proceedings — the outcome could ripple across the spyware industry. Stay safe out there.
