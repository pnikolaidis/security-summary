Good morning. I'm Allie with your Monday security briefing. Today's digest covers an actively exploited VPN vulnerability, two public-exploit router bugs, a controversial curl disclosure blackout, a major FCC privacy proposal, and a high-profile breach claim against a European intergovernmental body.

Our first story is urgent. Palo Alto Networks has confirmed that an unknown threat actor is actively exploiting a vulnerability in PAN-OS GlobalProtect VPN — the gateway software many organizations rely on for remote access. The flaw, tracked as CVE-2026-0257, is an authentication bypass with a CVSS score of 7.8, affecting both the portal and gateway components. That means attackers may be slipping past login entirely to reach your corporate network. Palo Alto has released patches, and given that exploitation is happening right now, this should be treated as a top priority for any team running GlobalProtect. If you haven't patched yet, today is the day.

Moving on to a pair of vulnerabilities in a popular travel router. GL.iNet's GL-MT3000 — a compact router favored by remote workers and privacy-conscious users — has two remotely exploitable command injection flaws, tracked as CVE-2026-12186 and CVE-2026-12187. Both carry a CVSS score of 8.8. The first targets the router's Tor proxy configuration handler; the second hits the online firmware upgrade mechanism. In both cases, an unauthenticated attacker can execute arbitrary commands on the device from the network. Worse, proof-of-concept exploit code is already publicly available. The good news: the vendor responded quickly and released a fix in firmware version 4.7. If you or anyone you support is running this device on firmware 4.4.5 or earlier, update immediately.

Our third story is a different kind of security news — a policy announcement from the curl project that's sparked real debate. Daniel Stenberg, curl's lead maintainer, announced that the project will not accept vulnerability reports during the month of July 2026. He's calling it a summer of bliss — a rest period for the maintainer team, which has handled a relentless stream of security disclosures. The announcement drew nearly 450 upvotes and 176 comments on Hacker News, with researchers debating what responsible disclosers should do if they find a critical bug during the blackout. Curl is one of the most widely deployed software libraries on the internet — it's embedded in billions of devices. The question of whether a voluntary disclosure moratorium creates a window for attackers, or simply sets a healthy precedent for open-source maintainer burnout, is genuinely unsettled.

Story four is a privacy and surveillance policy story. The Federal Communications Commission is proposing rules that would effectively eliminate anonymous prepaid phones — so-called burner phones — in the United States. The proposal would require telecoms to collect and store government-issued identification from all customers, including prepaid subscribers. The FCC is framing this as an anti-scam measure, but the scope is much broader than that: it would apply to all new and renewing customers, and the collected data could be used by law enforcement for a wide range of purposes. Privacy advocates and civil liberties groups are raising the alarm, comparing the approach to telecom ID mandates in authoritarian states. For security professionals, the implications touch on journalist safety, whistleblower protection, and the opsec calculus for anyone who relies on phone number separation. The proposal is in its public comment period now.

And finally, the ShinyHunters extortion group is claiming to have breached the Council of Europe, alleging theft of 297 gigabytes of data that includes employee personal information. The Council of Europe is a major intergovernmental organization covering 46 member states, focused on human rights and the rule of law. ShinyHunters is a known and credible threat actor — they've previously been confirmed behind breaches at Ticketmaster and Carnival Cruise Line. The group is threatening to release the data publicly, which suggests they're using it as leverage in ransom negotiations. No official statement from the Council of Europe had been issued at the time of this digest.

That's your digest for Monday, June 15th. Patch GlobalProtect, update your GL.iNet routers if you run them, and keep an eye on the ShinyHunters situation for official confirmation from the Council of Europe. Stay safe out there.
