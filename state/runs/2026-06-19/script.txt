Good morning. Here is your security digest for June 19th, 2026. Today's headlines are dominated by active exploitation, critical patches, and a large-scale credential leak that puts tens of thousands of organizations on the clock.

Story one. CISA has added a Splunk Enterprise vulnerability to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog and given federal agencies just three days to patch — until this Sunday. The flaw is CVE-2026-20253, a missing authentication issue that allows unauthenticated attackers to execute arbitrary code remotely on Splunk Enterprise. This vulnerability was only disclosed eight days ago, and attackers were already exploiting it in the wild within days of disclosure. Splunk is widely used as a security monitoring platform, which makes it a particularly attractive target — compromising a SIEM can blind an organization to the breach that's already underway. If your organization runs Splunk Enterprise in a customer-facing or internet-adjacent configuration, treat this as an emergency patch.

Story two. F5 has shipped emergency, out-of-band security updates for two critical vulnerabilities in NGINX Open Source. The most severe, CVE-2026-42530, scores a nine-point-two on the CVSS version four scale and lives in NGINX's HTTP3 module. A remote attacker with no credentials can trigger a use-after-free condition by sending specially crafted traffic and achieve code execution. Because NGINX underpins a significant fraction of the world's web servers and API gateways, the potential exposure here is enormous. F5 skipped its normal release schedule to get these patches out, which tells you something about the urgency they're assigning to this. Administrators running NGINX Open Source should apply the update immediately, and especially check whether HTTP3 is enabled on any public-facing systems.

Story three. A security researcher has documented roughly ten thousand GitHub repositories that are actively distributing Trojan malware. This is not a theoretical supply-chain concern — it is an ongoing campaign. The repositories are designed to look like legitimate open-source tools and libraries, exploiting developers' default trust in GitHub as a distribution channel. The post landed on Hacker News and generated over eight hundred upvotes and more than two hundred comments, making it the most engaged-with security story in the community today. The scale suggests automation. If your team clones third-party repositories, this is worth a direct conversation about vetting practices.

Story four. CISA published an ICS advisory for a critical flaw in AVer PTC-series conference cameras — the kind you find in government meeting rooms, hospital conference centers, and commercial offices. CVE-2026-40624 scores a nine-point-eight out of ten. A remote, unauthenticated attacker can send a single crafted web request and achieve arbitrary code execution. Every deployed version is listed as affected — there is no safe version. These cameras are used across government services, healthcare, and commercial facilities worldwide. In practice, a compromised conference camera gives an attacker both an audio and visual foothold and a pivot point deeper into the network. Check your inventory for AVer PTC hardware and monitor the vendor's security page for firmware updates.

Story five. The security community is tracking a large-scale credential leak known as FortiBleed. Estimates place the exposure between seventy-three thousand and eighty-six thousand Fortinet firewall and VPN devices worldwide, covering FortiGate appliances and SSL-VPN gateways. The leaked dataset includes credentials for these devices and is already circulating broadly online. CISA issued a hardening advisory this week with specific steps: terminate all active VPN sessions immediately, reset all Fortinet passwords, upgrade to the PBKDF2 password hashing algorithm available in FortiOS seven-point-two-point-eleven and later, review your authentication and firewall logs for signs of lateral movement, and require phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication on every external-facing interface. The cause of the original exposure has not been confirmed, but the credential dump is real and accessible. Assume the affected credentials are already in attacker hands.

That's your digest for June 19th. Two active exploitation situations requiring emergency patches, a critical camera flaw in government facilities, a major credential leak, and a developer supply-chain campaign worth watching. Stay safe out there.
