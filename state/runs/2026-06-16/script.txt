Good morning. Here is your security digest for June sixteenth, twenty twenty-six. Today is a busy one — two actively exploited vulnerabilities hit the federal patch-mandatory list, three more are being chained in attacks, and a supply chain hit the Arch Linux ecosystem minutes before this digest was assembled.

Story one. CISA added two vulnerabilities to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog yesterday, and both are already being actively exploited in the wild. The first is a flaw in Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN Manager, tracked as CVE-2026-20262. An authenticated attacker on the network can use it to write arbitrary files and eventually escalate to root. Cisco confirmed it was being used in zero-day attacks before a patch was available. The second is a privilege escalation bug in the LiteSpeed cPanel plugin, CVE-2026-54420, with a severity score of eight point five out of ten. Exploiting it also leads to root-level access on web hosting servers running cPanel with the LiteSpeed plugin installed. Federal civilian agencies have until June eighteenth — that's this Wednesday — to apply patches for both. If you run Cisco SD-WAN infrastructure or manage cPanel hosting environments, those patches should be your first task today.

Story two. Fortinet's FortiSandbox platform is under active attack from three separate vulnerabilities at once. The threat intelligence firm Defused Cyber reported observing exploitation of CVE-2026-39813, CVE-2026-39808, and CVE-2026-25089 all within a twenty-four-hour window. What makes this especially notable is that FortiSandbox is a security product — it's the tool enterprises use to detonate suspicious files and identify threats. One of these three CVEs was only patched last week. Attackers moving this quickly to exploit newly released patches, against a security detection platform of all targets, underscores the shrinking window between patch release and active exploitation. FortiSandbox administrators should apply all available patches immediately and review recent detections for signs of compromise.

Story three. Researchers at Varonis Threat Labs have disclosed a three-bug attack chain they call SearchLeak that could have allowed anyone with a malicious link to steal emails, calendar data, OneDrive files, SharePoint documents, and even multi-factor authentication codes from a Microsoft 365 Copilot Enterprise user. All the victim had to do was click a link that appeared to point to a legitimate Microsoft domain. Behind the scenes, the link triggered prompt injection inside Copilot, which then silently searched the victim's data and exfiltrated it in a single request. Microsoft has patched all three underlying flaws, so no immediate action is required beyond ensuring your Microsoft 365 environment is fully updated. The broader lesson here is that AI assistants integrated into enterprise platforms create a new class of attack surface — anything that can be prompted can potentially be weaponized, and SearchLeak is a concrete proof of that.

Story four. A supply chain attack hit the Arch Linux AUR — the Arch User Repository — this morning, with roughly fifteen hundred packages reportedly compromised by a campaign researchers are calling Atomic Arch. AUR is the community-maintained package repository that Arch and derivative Linux distribution users rely on for software not in the official repos. Arch Linux responded by suspending new account registrations to prevent additional malicious uploads while the investigation continues. If you or your team use Arch Linux or an AUR-dependent distribution and recently installed packages from AUR, it's worth auditing what was installed and checking for unexpected processes or network connections. The investigation is ongoing and the full scope of the compromise is not yet known.

Story five. ESET researchers have discovered that SprySOCKS — a backdoor previously believed to target only Linux systems and linked to a Chinese threat actor — has expanded to Windows. Two new Windows variants have been identified, internally named WIN-DRV and WIN-PLUS. The Windows builds use kernel driver techniques for stealth, making them significantly harder to detect than standard malware. Attacks using these Windows variants have already been confirmed against government organizations in at least four countries. This kind of cross-platform expansion suggests active, well-resourced development behind the toolkit. Organizations in the government, defense, and critical infrastructure sectors should check their endpoint detection rules for SprySOCKS indicators and ensure kernel-level rootkit detection is enabled.

That's your digest. A lot happened overnight — two KEV additions, Fortinet under simultaneous three-CVE attack, a novel AI assistant exploit, a Linux supply chain hit, and a Chinese APT expanding its Windows footprint. Stay safe out there.
